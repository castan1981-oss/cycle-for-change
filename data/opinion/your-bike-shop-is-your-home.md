Not all bike shops are created equal. I want to say that first, because it's true, and because this isn't a love letter to every shop with a repair stand.

But think about what you do when something goes wrong. You order the wrong size. Your derailleur breaks down. Where do you go? You go to the bike shop. They fix it. And a lot of shops do that for a very small price compared to what it costs to keep the doors open.

That's what I want to talk about. Because some of the biggest brands in cycling are changing how they treat those shops, and I don't think most riders know it.

## What a shop actually is

Every bike shop I've ever walked into has a feeling. An emotion. Something about it that's amazing.

I've been to a lot of different shops. I never felt quite like I did when I went to Bicycle Haüs in Scottsdale. Not because they're superior or anything like that. It's that when they spoke to me, they told me the truth. When they fixed my bike, most of the time they told me I didn't need all the things I asked for. And it cost less than I figured it would.

That's what a good shop is. They're not there to give you free stuff. They're there to give you solid advice, make sure your bike is safe, and make a living.

The mechanics, the owners, the reps who come by with new products. All of those people have jobs. In the US there are about 12,000 bicycle repairers on payroll, and their median pay is around $20.50 an hour [1]. Nobody is getting rich turning wrenches.

## The bike sale isn't a windfall

I used to think new bikes were where a shop made its money. It's more complicated than that, and honestly it makes the case stronger.

New bikes are the biggest piece of what most shops sell. Back in 2013, when the National Bicycle Dealers Association broke it down, bikes were 46% of a typical shop's revenue. But the margin on those bikes was about 35%, which was below what the average shop needed just to break even. Parts, accessories and service are where the profit lived [2].

The bike margin kept shrinking. The NBDA's 2022 study, which covered 2020 and 2021, found new-bike margins at typical dealers were down 9 points from a decade earlier, and bikes ranked fifth among a shop's profit centers. Shops still made record profits in those boom years, mostly by keeping costs down [3]. It didn't last. In the NBDA's 2022–23 survey, a typical shop's net profit was about 4.7% before taxes [4].

So the bike sale isn't where a shop gets rich. It's what brings you through the door. You buy the bike there, you get fit there, you come back for the tune-up, the tires, the bar tape, the advice. When the bike sale goes somewhere else, the whole relationship starts thinner. And a shop running on margins like that doesn't have much room to lose.

## What some brands are doing

Here's where it gets frustrating.

Specialized started selling bikes direct in February 2022. Some shipped straight to your door with no shop involved. If a dealer built one of those bikes for pickup, it got 50% of its normal margin. If it delivered the bike to your door, 75% [5]. Today Specialized's own FAQ says every online order has to ship to an authorized retailer. But S-Works and Turbo bikes can only go to retailers authorized for those lines [6]. So not every Specialized dealer can take every Specialized order.

Trek announced in July 2024 that it would ship bikes to people's homes. Under that program, the nearest partner dealer got a 15 to 20% margin on those sales. On an online order picked up from the shop's own stock, the regular margin was 27 to 39% [7]. Trek's president, John Burke, says the company owns around 400 stores worldwide [9]. In June 2026, Bicycle Retailer reported that Trek had started filling some online orders out of those company stores [8]. One shop owner told Cycling West a Trek rep offered them a dealership even though a Trek store was a mile and a half away. The rep's answer: "It doesn't matter, it's a corporate store." [10]

That's the division I'm talking about. The brand is your supplier and your competitor at the same time.

And it's not just those two. Rick Vosper, who has covered this business for years at Bicycle Retailer, wrote this year that "virtually all suppliers now sell consumer-direct" and that dealer trust in some big brands is at "an all-time low." He also reported that Trek, Specialized and Pon (the company behind Cannondale, Santa Cruz and Cervélo) now run retail chains with many hundreds of locations between them [11].

Meanwhile shops are closing, though nobody has a clean count. About 650 shops that were in brand dealer locators at the end of 2023 weren't in them a year later. Not all of those closed. Some were bought, renamed or moved, and about 770 new shops showed up over the same year. Vosper's working guess was 500 or more real closures, against a couple hundred in a normal year before COVID [12]. He has also written that the die-off may be more perception than reality [13]. When Bicycle Sports in Beaumont, Texas closed after 41 years, the owner said every brand they carried also sells direct online, and that "the margins are razor thin." [14]

## They still need the shop

Here's the part that gets me. These brands still rely on shops for almost everything that happens after the sale.

Giant ships online bikes only to a local retailer for assembly [15]. Cannondale won't ship a bike to your house at all. It goes to a shop [16]. Giant and Tern both say warranty work has to go through an authorized dealer [17] [18]. So the shop builds the bike, handles the warranty and fixes it when it breaks. Sometimes for less than it would have made selling it.

And look at what happens when there's no shop in the picture. When YT Industries' US arm shut down in October 2025, US warranty support wasn't back in place until March 2026 [19]. Ride1Up only covers warranty labor in the first 30 days, then the owner pays [20]. Some shops told Bicycle Retailer they now turn away direct-to-consumer e-bikes altogether. They pointed to safety, insurance and battery fire rules, and one said parts from online brands take two months or longer to show up [21].

You can skip the shop when you buy. You can't skip it when something breaks.

## The fair part

I want to be honest about the other side.

Buying direct can be cheaper. Back in 2018, Outside reported Canyon's prices running 10 to 40% below the competition [22]. A lot of people want to buy online, and brands didn't make that up. Some brand programs do pay shops for building and delivering bikes. Cannondale and Giant still send every online bike through a shop, which tells me this can be done in a way that takes care of everyone.

And bad shop experiences are real. People have been talked down to [23]. Women have said they felt patronized [24]. Not all shops are created equal, and the bad ones hurt all of them.

Sure, some shops sell ridiculously high-priced bikes. People love to buy those. I'm one of them.

I'm not saying never buy anything online. I'm saying know who you're buying from.

## What I'm asking

Your bike shop is your home, especially if you're a cyclist. It's where you get the truth about your bike. It's where somebody tells you it's safe to ride, and tells you when it isn't.

I'm not saying buy a bike there because people have jobs. What I'm saying is that in order for us to keep the culture and the things we have right now, we need to get away from brands that aren't taking care of bike shops.

Before you buy your next bike, look at how that brand treats the shop that's going to fix it. Does the bike go through a shop? Does the shop get paid fairly for the work? Is the brand opening a store down the street from the dealer that built its name?

Then buy from your shop when you can. Pay for the tune-up. Take the advice, especially when they tell you that you don't need the thing you came in for.

That's the shop worth keeping.
