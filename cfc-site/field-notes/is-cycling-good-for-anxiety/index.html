Is Cycling Good for Anxiety?

Cycling helps my anxiety. Not every time, and not like a prescription — but consistently enough that I've stopped thinking of it as exercise and started thinking of it as maintenance. Research on aerobic exercise and anxiety symptoms points the same direction. Here's what I've noticed from the road.

Is cycling good for anxiety? What I've learned from the miles

I ride most mornings. Some of those mornings I don't want to. Anxiety has a way of making the couch feel safer than the road, which is exactly backwards from what actually helps.

The first ten minutes are usually rough. Legs waking up, heart rate climbing, brain still running whatever it was running before I got out of bed. I'm negotiating with myself the whole way through the neighborhood.

Then something shifts. It isn't dramatic. Somewhere around mile three or four, the noise goes quieter.

What the road does to the spiral

Part of what cycling does is demand your attention in a specific way. A descent, a gap in traffic, a turn you have to judge — your eyes are out there, not in here. It's hard to run a full anxiety loop when the road is requiring something from you.

That's not the same as meditating. I'm not empty out there. But the repetitive, physical focus of riding — the cadence, the wind, the body doing its work — uses the same cognitive space the spiral runs on. There isn't room for both at full volume.

The noise doesn't disappear. It just has to compete with the road.

Public health research consistently links regular aerobic exercise — cycling included — to reduced anxiety symptoms. That tracks with what I've experienced across a lot of miles. It's not a cure. It's a tool that works when I use it.

What it won't do

It won't resolve whatever the anxiety is about.

If there's a real conversation to have, a real decision sitting unresolved — it'll be there when I get back. The ride doesn't move it. What it does is change the angle I return to it from. A little tired in a good way. Head quieter. More able to sit with it instead of spin around it.

That's different from solving it. But it matters.

Riding for something bigger on the hard days

I'm logging 7,500 miles — swimming, cycling, running — for queer communities by 2027. That mission is part of what gets me out the door on mornings when anxiety says to stay in.

There's a difference between "I said I'd ride today" and "this mile is for someone." On the hard days, the second one wins. Accountability to something outside yourself has a different pull than a plan you made for yourself on a better morning.
