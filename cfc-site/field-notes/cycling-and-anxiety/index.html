What Cycling Does for Anxiety

Cycling won't cure anxiety. But it changes the math. An hour on the bike is an hour where the noise has to compete with the road — and the road usually wins. That's what I've learned from riding toward 7,500 miles.

Is cycling good for anxiety?

The honest answer is: it helps me. I can't speak to anyone else's brain chemistry, and I'm not a therapist. I'm someone who has lived with anxiety for a long time and found that the bike does something the couch doesn't.

What happens physically is real. Your heart rate climbs. Your body has something to do. The cortisol that's been sitting in your chest for three days finally has somewhere to go. Exercise does that — cycling is just the version I keep showing up for.

Research on aerobic exercise and anxiety exists and is generally encouraging. But I'd be lying if I told you I got on the bike because I read a study.

What I notice on the bike

The anxious brain wants to replay. It wants to run the tape again, find the part where things went wrong, and stay there. The bike doesn't allow that for long.

When I'm climbing, I'm counting. Watching the grade. Watching my breathing. Watching whether the truck behind me is going to give me room. There's no bandwidth left for the spiral.

When I'm on a flat stretch with nothing to do but spin, the loop comes back. But even then it's different. You're moving. You're outside. The heat or the wind or the light is doing something to your body, and that physical fact is harder to ignore than your thoughts.

I don't know if that's neurological or just attention. I don't need to know. It works often enough that I keep going.

The catch

The catch is you have to get out the door. That's the whole game with anxiety and exercise. The days I most need to ride are the days it's hardest to clip in and leave.

The only thing that works for me: small goals get me out when big ones don't. "I'll just ride to the end of the street" has started more good rides than any motivational framing I've ever tried. Once you're moving, moving is easier.

The other catch is that anxiety doesn't disappear. It gets quieter for a while. For some people, with consistency, it gets a lot quieter over time. But the bike isn't a cure. Framing it that way sets up a bad dynamic — a hard week starts to feel like failure, and it isn't. It's a hard week. You ride when you can.

The line doesn't ask how you felt when you started. It only counts that you rode.

Why the miles matter here

I'm riding 7,500 miles for queer communities. Queer people experience higher rates of anxiety and depression than the general population — not because of who we are, but because of what gets put in front of us.

The money pledged on this ride goes to organizations working on queer mental health: direct services, crisis lines, youth programs. The miles and the cause aren't separate. They're the same thing.

If this landed somewhere for you, consider pledging a mile. Every dollar goes to the orgs doing the work I can't do from a bike.

If you're struggling: the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is free and 24/7 — call or text 988. For LGBTQ youth, the Trevor Project is at 1-866-488-7386 (or text START to 678-678).
