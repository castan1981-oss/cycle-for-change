Does bike tire tread matter on pavement?

Whether tread adds grip on pavement is contested. Some riders and writers say it adds little or nothing, while Challenge, Vittoria and Rene Herse say it helps in the wet and Continental calls road tread largely aesthetic. No independent controlled test settles it, and most of the makers quoted by Velo rank rubber compound above tread. In independent rolling-resistance tests, a tire’s casing and compound often change speed more than its tread does. Choose tread for the loosest surface you’ll ride, then pick the best casing you can afford.

The evidence here is moderate: real tests, but not many of them. 8 sources, listed at the end.

What the tests say

Pavement (contested). Sheldon Brown’s long-standing explanation is that all tires get slippery on wet metal, paint and rail tracks, and tread features don’t help there1. Bicycles don’t realistically hydroplane, because a narrow, rounded contact patch pushes water aside1. He argues knobby treads grip worse on hard surfaces, since knobs bend under side loads while a smooth tread can’t1, and that fine “file” treads on road tires are mostly cosmetic or marketing1. Wikipedia’s summary agrees: on-road, a tread pattern “offers little to no improvement in traction”, and light tread is often added because of the mistaken belief that slicks are slippery in the wet2. Not everyone agrees. In a Velo technical FAQ, Specialized and Vittoria ranked compound above tread for road traction, but Specialized said “tread does have an influence on traction in road tires”, Vittoria said “a treaded design will deliver the best overall performance in wet conditions”, and Challenge said a slick works on dry roads but a herringbone tread helps once it is wet. Continental called tread patterns “largely aesthetic”7. Rene Herse argues tread matters on the road because it helps the tire interlock with the surface, though its evidence is anecdotal and historical rather than test data8. Pressure does change wet grip: BRR measured the average center-grip coefficient of three 28mm road tires rising from 0.69 at 108 psi to 0.78 at 54 psi (edge grip 0.72 to 0.77)6.

Rolling resistance: casing vs tread. Bicycle Rolling Resistance (BRR) runs tires on a diamond-plate drum (one tire, 28.8 km/h, 42.5 kg load) and lists results at each width’s “low” test pressure35. Three comparisons stand out:

  • Same brand, more tread. Schwalbe’s lower-tread G-One RS Pro 40 measured 15.7 W, and the knobbier G-One R Pro 40 measured 16.6 W. That’s 0.9 W for more tread3.
  • Same tread, different casing. Rene Herse’s Snoqualmie Pass 44 measured 13.5 W in its Extralight casing and 17.4 W in its standard casing. That’s 3.9 W from casing alone, about four times the tread difference above3.
  • Slick isn’t automatically fastest. Panaracer’s GravelKing Slick 40 measured 18.4 W, slower than both knobbed Schwalbe tires3. The comparison isn’t clean: it is a different casing, and BRR’s “low” test pressure differs (33 psi vs 36 psi).

Mountain bike tires show the same pattern. Two low-profile cross-country (XC) tires, Schwalbe’s Thunder Burt Super Race 2.35 (2026) and the 2023 Maxxis Aspen MaxxSpeed EXO/TR 2.4, measured 17.3 W and 29.9 W. The Maxxis had about 73% higher rolling resistance4. Maxxis’s 2024 Aspen ST MaxxSpeed EXO/TR Team Spec 2.4 measured 26.7 W (about 54% higher than the Schwalbe), so the gap depends on which Aspen variant is compared4. Schwalbe’s near-slick G-One Speed Pro 2.35 measured 11.9 W4.

Construction. Wikipedia notes that higher thread counts in the casing improve ride quality and lower rolling resistance, but cost durability and puncture resistance2. Tread rubbers mix natural and synthetic rubber with fillers such as carbon black and silica, and some tires use a dual compound that is harder in the middle and grippier on the edges2.

Off-road. Here tread does real work: knobs bite into loose surfaces. Some MTB tires are designed specifically for the front or the rear, and some treads are directional2. But BRR’s drum says nothing about traction on loose gravel, sand or mud, and its fixed speed and load don’t match every rider35. No independent, controlled off-road traction tests were found. Advice on loose-vs-hardpack gravel treads and MTB knob layouts (center knobs for rolling and braking, shoulder knobs for cornering) comes mainly from manufacturers and rider experience. Treat it as informed opinion.

Desert angle. On thorny desert routes, how well the casing resists punctures is a separate question from tread. That’s covered.

The numbers

  • 0.9 W between Schwalbe G-One R Pro 40 (16.6 W) and the lower-tread G-One RS Pro 40 (15.7 W)3
  • 3.9 W between the same Rene Herse tread in Extralight (13.5 W) vs standard (17.4 W) casing3
  • 18.4 W: Panaracer GravelKing Slick 40, slower than the knobbed G-One R and RS3
  • 17.3 W vs 29.9 W: two low-profile XC MTB tires (Thunder Burt Super Race 2026 vs 2023 Aspen MaxxSpeed); the 2024 Aspen Team Spec measured 26.7 W4
  • BRR test conditions: one tire, 28.8 km/h, 42.5 kg load, diamond-plate drum5
  • Wet center-grip coefficient 0.78 at 54 psi vs 0.69 at 108 psi, average of three 28mm road tires6

Myths and disagreements

  • “Slicks are dangerous in the rain” vs “tread doesn’t matter on pavement.” Both are contested. Makers disagree on whether road tread helps in the wet, most rank compound above tread, and no controlled test settles it78. Lower pressure raised wet grip in BRR’s test6.
  • “Bike tires can hydroplane.” Not at realistic speeds: the rounded, narrow contact patch clears water18.
  • “Knobs are slow, slicks are fast.” Casing and compound can outweigh tread, and one slick tested slower than knobbed tires34.
  • Disagreement: off-road tread choice. Manufacturer advice on loose vs hardpack treads and knob layouts isn’t backed by independent traction data that could be found.

Choosing tread for your riding

  • For road riding, a slick or fine-tread tire is the usual choice. Spend your money on a supple, fast casing and a good compound, which independent tests show matter for speed37.
  • For mixed gravel, more tread costs relatively little on pavement (under 1 W in the Schwalbe pair BRR tested), so choose tread for the worst surface you’ll actually ride3.
  • Compare tires on BRR’s numbers, where test conditions are the same for every tire, rather than by eye. Similar-looking tires can be far apart34.
  • On MTB tires marked front- or rear-specific or directional, mount them as marked2.
