Are aero wheels worth it?

Aero wheels help, but published figures run from seconds to about a minute over 40 km, depending on what is compared. One tunnel test of five aero wheelsets implies about 16 s per 40 km from best to worst, a power-model estimate puts shallow-to-deep rims at about 30 s per hour at 40 km/h, and a 2001 model puts an aerodynamic wheelset 60–82 s ahead of a conventional one. The wrong tire can cancel a wheel’s lead, and from January 2026 UCI mass-start road races cap rims at 65mm deep.

The evidence here is thin: few independent tests, so treat the numbers as a starting point. 15 sources, listed at the end.

What the tests say

Why wheels matter. Wheels are only about 10% of total aerodynamic drag, a figure a 2023 TU Delft paper quotes from Greenwell et al. (1995)15. Air drag takes most of a rider’s effort on the flat1.

Published time savings.

  • Jeukendrup & Martin’s peer-reviewed model (2001) puts an aerodynamic wheelset 60–82 s faster over a 40 km time trial than a conventional set. It is a 2001 model, read at abstract level11.
  • Inside Triathlon’s 2012 tunnel test (Scottsdale; bike only) predicted drag for five aero clincher sets from 757 g (best) to 796 g. By the tunnel engineer’s rule (100 g of drag = 1 s per km), the best saved about 70 s over the 112-mile Ironman bike leg12. That is about 16 s per 40 km (our conversion: 0.39 s/km × 40 km), an aero-versus-aero comparison, not aero versus conventional.
  • A power-model estimate in Cyclist: shallow to deep rims at 40 km/h is worth around 10 W, or 30 s over the hour13.

Tunnel data and tires. Hunt’s 2019 test of front wheels alone (GST Windkanal, 45 km/h, ±20° yaw, 25mm tire) ran from 15.74 W (Zipp 202 NSW) to 19.74 W (Mavic Cosmic Elite UST), a 4 W spread among shallow-to-mid wheels. It is a manufacturer test with stated ±2.5 W repeatability. A 28mm tire instead of 25mm added 1.54 W on the Hunt 34 Aero Wide Disc (inside that error) and 4.39 W on the Zipp (outside it), enough to erase the Zipp’s 1 W lead2. SILCA’s “rule of 105” says the rim should be at least 105% of the tire’s width3, but AeroCoach’s 2025 chart of nine deep wheels showed “no simple relationship” (highest- and lowest-drag wheels: 112% and 113%)4. A TU Delft tunnel study of a 62mm wheel at 45 km/h reported a 33% wind-averaged drag difference between tires, the extreme being one slick tire with and without its centre seam15.

Yaw and crosswinds. About 80% of riding time at 45 km/h is spent at 7.5° yaw or less4. Roues Artisanales’ 2008 test found deep, low-spoke-count rims best, and flat wide-bladed spokes “behave as parachutes”5. A Monash tunnel study (50 km/h, 24–85mm rims plus discs, 0–30° yaw) found wheel type “has a significant effect on both the aerodynamic drag and stability” of the bicycle system, and a disc gave no large drag gain over deep-section wheels at small yaw; per-wheel drag values appear only in its figures14. BikeRadar’s reviewers found a 54mm wheel demanding in crosswinds, a subjective view6. From 1 January 2026, the UCI caps rim height at 65mm for mass-start road racing78.

Weight vs aero on climbs. Roues Artisanales found a much lighter wheelset gives a 70 kg rider about 7 W over a 10-second 0–30 km/h sprint9. Cyclist quotes a wheelmaker citing earlier Cervélo testing that aero gains outweigh weight up to about a 5% gradient for an average rider (8% for a pro), and a model putting a 0.5 kg penalty at under 5 W on a 10% climb13. These are expert statements, not tests.

Wide rims. At the 2026 Tour, some Visma-Lease a Bike wheels were so wide that 29mm was the narrowest ETRTO-approved tire10.

The numbers

  • 76% (32 km/h) and 83% (40 km/h) of a 90 kg rider’s effort goes to aero drag on the flat (Wikipedia figure; not re-checked)1
  • 60–82 s per 40 km: modelled gain of an aero wheelset over a conventional 2001 set11
  • About 70 s over 112 miles (about 16 s per 40 km, our conversion): best vs worst of five aero wheelsets, bike only12
  • About 10 W, or 30 s per hour at 40 km/h: shallow to deep rims, modelled13
  • 15.74–19.74 W aerodynamic power across five front wheels at 45 km/h, ±20° yaw (Hunt, 2019)2
  • +1.54 W to +4.39 W from switching a 25mm to a 28mm tire on the same wheel; Hunt quotes ±2.5 W repeatability2
  • 33% difference in wind-averaged drag between tires (one slick tire with vs without its seam), 62mm wheel15
  • 65mm maximum rim height, UCI mass-start road, from 1 January 20267

Myths and disagreements

  • “Deep wheels save minutes.” Among modern shallow-to-deep wheels the data point to seconds over 40 km21213. Only the model against a 2001 conventional set reaches 60–82 s11.
  • “Any carbon wheel is aero.” Rim shape, spokes and the tire-rim match matter, and a carbon wheel lost its edge with a wider tire24515.
  • “Light always beats aero on climbs.” Weight matters most in accelerations9. The 5%/8% crossover gradients are expert claims, not published tests13.
  • Disagreement: yaw. Some tests weight wide yaw ranges (±20°, 0–35°), while AeroCoach argues most riding happens at low yaw245. Wheels that look good at high yaw may not help most riders much.

Deciding on aero wheels

  • Fix position and tires first. Those numbers are larger than most wheel swaps (see,,).
  • Ask for the test conditions behind any aero claim: speed, yaw range, bike only or with rider, front wheel or pair, tire width.
  • Run the tire width the wheel maker optimized for, and check what it measures once mounted234.
  • Choose a front rim depth you can control in your usual winds. Gusty open desert roads favor caution614.
  • Racing UCI mass-start road events? Rims must be 65mm or shallower from 2026, and many current aero wheelsets were already near 60mm78.
