How to prevent flat bike tires

For goathead country, the most practical defenses are tubeless with fresh sealant, or, if you stay with tubes, a liner plus thorn-resistant tubes. In lab tests, liners vary enormously: one roughly doubled puncture resistance for under 2 W, while the most protective cost almost 14 W. Run enough pressure, or an insert, to avoid pinch flats. Nobody has published real-world puncture rates, so nobody can honestly promise a percentage reduction.

The evidence here is moderate: real tests, but not many of them. 13 sources, listed at the end.

What the tests say

What causes flats. Sheldon Brown groups them into three causes1:

  • punctures from glass, wire and thorns
  • pinch flats (“snakebites”): two small holes from hitting an edge while underinflated
  • rim tape failures that expose spoke holes

He names goathead thorns as a notable hazard in the Southwestern US. He recommends liners, “thornproof” tubes with a thicker outer wall, or sealant1.

Goatheads. Goathead, or puncture vine (Tribulus terrestris), is native to warm parts of southern Eurasia and Africa and has been introduced to North America2. Each fruit splits into five burs, each armed with two large spines and sometimes two smaller ones12; Wikipedia gives a spine length of about 10mm2. Extension and weed-board sources say the burs puncture bicycle tires1213. Seeds can remain viable in the soil for up to five years1112, and one city summary says 5–7 years or longer13. Wikipedia lists the plant as a noxious weed in some US states and recommends removal before or during flowering2. Nobody has studied how often goatheads cause flats.

Liners (BRR lab test). Bicycle Rolling Resistance tested liners in a Schwalbe Kojak 35mm tire at 60 psi. The bare tire scored 73 points for tread puncture resistance3:

Liner Puncture score Extra rolling resistance Weight
Mr Tuffy 136 +1.9 W 63g
Mr Tuffy Ultralite 125 +1.7 W 61g
Tannus Armour 245 +13.6 W 221g
Panaracer FlatAway 126 +14.4 W 29g

Three of the four liners also cut wet grip by 5–6 points; the FlatAway left it unchanged. BRR judged both Mr Tuffy liners the best balance, and said Tannus Armour “will prevent virtually all punctures” at a big speed cost3.

Tubeless and inserts. Going tubeless removes the inner tube, which is what gets pinched in a classic snakebite, and lets sealant close small holes. It’s also faster: across eight gravel tires, tubeless averaged 3.9 W less than butyl tubes at low pressure4. In BRR’s insert test, a 40mm tubeless tire without an insert failed at 9 psi, running on the rim edges. With a CushCore (122g) or Vittoria AirLiner (56g) insert, it completed the test. Both inserts added little rolling resistance down to about 27 psi5. Sealant has to be kept fresh, especially in heat (see). Stan’s is reported to say its sealant stays liquid for 2–6 months depending on temperature and humidity6.

Tubes. In BRR’s 2026 update, latex was the fastest tube material (9.5 W at 100 psi). The best TPU tubes were close behind (9.7 W), and standard butyl was slower (10.5–12.4 W)7. BRR didn’t systematically measure TPU puncture resistance, so claims that TPU tubes resist punctures better are untested7.

Casing and belts. Sheldon Brown says puncture belts add a little weight and rolling resistance, but “probably have some value” against hazards like broken glass8. BRR’s road-tire tests report a “Total Puncture Score” that combines sharp and blunt puncture force with total tire thickness, which makes tires directly comparable9.

Pressure and pinch flats. Berto’s 15% tire-drop rule and wider tires both reduce how far the tire squashes, and so cut snakebite risk10. Sheldon names underinflation as a major cause of snakebites1.

The numbers

  • Goathead burs carry two large spines (sometimes two smaller ones); seeds can stay viable in soil up to 5 years, and 5–7 years or longer by one account111213
  • Mr Tuffy liner: puncture score 73 → 136 for +1.9 W3
  • Tannus Armour: 73 → 245, but +13.6 W and 221g3
  • Panaracer FlatAway: +14.4 W, the largest penalty tested3
  • Gravel tubeless vs butyl: −3.9 W per tire at low pressure4
  • Without an insert, a 40mm tubeless tire failed at 9 psi. With an insert, it held5
  • Tubes at 100 psi: latex 9.5 W, best TPU 9.7 W, butyl 10.5–12.4 W7

Myths and disagreements

  • “Tubeless means no flats.” Sealant dries out, and large cuts or burps still happen56.
  • “Liners are basically free.” Penalties ranged from +1.7 W to +14.4 W3.
  • “TPU tubes are more puncture-resistant.” Not measured by BRR7.
  • Gap: real-world rates. Lab puncture scores use a fixed probe39. There’s no field data comparing puncture rates for tubeless, tubes, liners or tire models.

Stopping flats

  • Desert gravel or MTB: run tubeless with fresh sealant (BRR’s gravel tests used 30ml per tire) and check it about monthly in summer. Carry plugs and a spare tube46.
  • Staying with tubes in goathead areas: use a thornproof tube plus a Mr Tuffy-type liner, the best trade-off in BRR’s test13.
  • Rocky terrain at low pressure: consider an insert5.
  • Pick tires by puncture score: compare protected and race casings on BRR’s road and gravel puncture scores, not marketing names9.
  • After a goathead stretch: stop and check your tires. Pulling a thorn out of a tubeless tire lets the sealant work. That’s practical advice, not a tested method.
