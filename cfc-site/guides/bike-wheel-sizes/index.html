Bike wheel sizes explained: 700c, 650b, 29 and 26

Wheel “sizes” are really rim bead-seat diameters: 622mm (700c and 29”), 584mm (650b and 27.5”), 571mm (650c) and 559mm (26”). A tire’s width changes the outside diameter as much as the rim does, so by the standard formula a 650b x 47 tire is about the same height as a 700 x 28, not a 700 x 40, as WTB, which launched the size, also says. Bigger wheels roll over obstacles more easily and smaller ones are stiffer and quicker to spin up, but four mountain-bike studies (9 to 55 riders) split on whether 29” is faster, and none cover road or gravel sizes.

The evidence here is moderate: real tests, but not many of them. 10 sources, listed at the end.

What the tests say

What the numbers mean. ISO/ETRTO sizing uses bead seat diameter (BSD), where the tire bead sits on the rim:

  • 622mm: road “700c”, hybrids and “29-inch” MTBs (“29er” is a marketing name for wide 622mm tires)
  • 584mm: 650b, sold on MTBs as 27.5”
  • 571mm: 650c, for triathlon/TT bikes and smaller road riders
  • 559mm: most 26” MTBs1

Outer diameter. Total diameter is roughly BSD + 2 × tire height2. Using nominal width as a stand-in for height (calculated here; real tire heights run a little lower and vary):

Tire Approx. outer diameter
650b x 47 584 + 94 = 678mm
700 x 28 622 + 56 = 678mm
700 x 40 622 + 80 = 702mm

So 650b x 47 matches a 700 x 28, not a 700 x 40. WTB, which launched the 650b x 47 Horizon “Road Plus” tire, describes it as having “the same overall wheel diameter as a 700 x 28c tire”6. To match a 700 x 40, a 650b tire would need to be about 59mm (2.3”) wide. Swapping a 700 x 40 for a 650b x 47 would lower the bottom bracket by roughly 12mm. For road racing, the UCI caps wheels at 700mm including the tire. By Elite Wheels’ figures that leaves about 38–39mm nominal on a 622 rim (38mm ≈ 697mm, 39mm ≈ 699mm), and a 40mm tire comes to about 701mm2.

Stiffness and weight. Damon Rinard’s lateral stiffness tests found 650c wheels about 25% stiffer than 700c on equivalent hubs3. Wikipedia’s 29er article cites Mavic engineers saying 29” wheels were 39% less stiff and 10% heavier than a similar 26” wheel. That’s a secondhand manufacturer claim4.

Rollover vs acceleration. The usual trade-off is that larger wheels roll over obstacles more easily and carry momentum on rough ground, while being heavier, slower to accelerate and harder to fit on small frames4. Roues Artisanales measured how much energy it takes to accelerate wheels. An 820g wheelset needed 74 J and a 1,973g wheelset 141 J. For a 70 kg rider on a 4.1 kg bike, that difference is worth about 7 W over a 10-second sprint from 0 to 30 km/h5.

Mountain-bike trials: mixed. Ten Swiss national-team riders on a simulated cross-country course were faster on a 29” bike than a 26” (304s vs 311s, about 2%) at similar power7. In 55 cyclists on a 2,110m circuit, 29” was faster but transmitted more vibration and raised heart rate, with similar power9. Nine trained riders lapping a 3.48km course on 26”, 27.5” and 29” showed no performance differences8. In 49 highly trained riders on short rocky climbs and descents, times didn’t differ, but 26” gave more vibration at the coccyx uphill and 29” more at wrists and ankles downhill10. Nobody has published a study of 650b vs 700c on road or gravel, or of mullet setups.

Mixed wheels and rider height. “96ers” ran a 29” front with a 26” rear, and Trek sold a 29”/26” bike in 20074. Today’s 29/27.5 “mullet” follows that idea. Toe overlap and standover are the usual concerns with 29ers for riders under about 5’5”4.

The numbers

  • Bead seat diameters: 622mm (700c/29”), 584mm (650b/27.5”), 571mm (650c), 559mm (26”)1
  • Approximate outer diameter = BSD + 2 × tire height2. So 650b x 47 ≈ 678mm ≈ 700 x 28, while 700 x 40 ≈ 702mm (calculated here)
  • UCI road maximum: 700mm total, about 38–39mm nominal tire on 622 rims2
  • 650c wheels were about 25% stiffer laterally than 700c (Rinard)3
  • 29” wheels 39% less stiff and 10% heavier than 26” (Mavic claim, via Wikipedia)4
  • About 7 W over a 10-second 0–30 km/h sprint, ultralight vs heavy wheelset5
  • Swiss national team (n = 10): 304s on 29” vs 311s on 26” over a simulated XC course7

Myths and disagreements

  • “650b x 47 = 700 x 40 in diameter.” By the standard formula it equals about 700 x 28. A 700 x 40 is about 24mm taller2.
  • “29ers are proven faster.” Two of four MTB studies (9 to 55 riders) found 29” faster on cross-country courses and two found no time difference. Two found more vibration on the 29” in some sections78910.
  • “Rotating weight transforms performance.” Roues Artisanales’ data shows a few watts, and only during hard accelerations5.
  • “27.5 is a different size from 650b.” They’re the same 584mm BSD1.

Picking a wheel size

  • To fit fatter gravel tires in the same frame, 650b x 47–50 can work. Expect a lower bottom bracket than with 700 x 40, and check the frame maker’s approved sizes2.
  • When you change wheel size or tire width, update your bike computer’s wheel circumference.
  • For MTB, choose by frame fit and terrain, and demo if you can. The studies don’t settle which size is faster78910.
  • Shorter riders: check toe overlap and standover before choosing 29” or 700c on small frames4.
